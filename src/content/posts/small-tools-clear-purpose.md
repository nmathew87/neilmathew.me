Some of the most useful software starts with a very small frustration: a task that takes five steps when it could take one, or a piece of information that is always just out of reach.

A small tool can make that moment easier without needing to become a platform.

## Start with the moment

Before thinking about features, describe the situation. What is someone trying to do? What interrupts them? What would a good result look like? Imagine someone collecting links for a project: each link gets copied from a browser, pasted into a note, given a short description, and eventually lost somewhere among a dozen other notes. The problem might seem to call for a whole new knowledge-management system, but the useful first step could be much smaller: a single place to save a link with one sentence about why it matters. Looking closely at that moment changes the questions you ask. Instead of wondering how many folders, tags, or views to build, you can ask whether saving the link takes too long, whether the description is easy to add, and whether the person can find it again a week later. Those are concrete things you can observe and improve. A small prototype gives you a way to test the idea in an ordinary working day, where interruptions and habits tend to reveal more than a carefully prepared demo. You may discover that search matters much more than organization, or that the simplest improvement is showing the last five things saved. The point is to let the actual experience shape the tool before a growing list of possibilities starts shaping it for you.

That description often gives you a more useful starting point than a list of technologies.

## Make the next step obvious

A focused tool should make its purpose clear through its interface. A place to put something, a way to act on it, and an understandable result can be enough.

> Small is a useful constraint when it helps you decide what belongs.

The interesting challenge is knowing where to stop. Each additional option may be useful on its own, but together they can obscure the simple thing that made the tool worth building.

## Leave space for the next idea

A first version can answer one question. Use it for a while, notice where it breaks down, and let those observations guide what comes next.

Sometimes the best next feature is a clearer label. Sometimes it is removing a step. And sometimes the tool is already enough.
